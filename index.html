Shallow corals acclimate to mesophotic depths while maintaining their heat tolerance against ongoing climate change

Tavakoli-Kolour P*, Sinniger F, Morita M, Hazraty-Kari S, Nakamura T, Harii S* (2024). Marine Pollution Bulletin, 209, 117277.

Research highlight
  • Shallow corals retained their heat tolerance after transplantation to cooler mesophotic depths.
  • Mesophotic-acclimated corals showed higher thermal thresholds and delayed bleaching.
  • Lower light intensity at depth may help modulate coral bleaching responses.
  • Mesophotic ecosystems may provide refugia for shallow corals in warming oceans.
DOI
Abstract

Global warming poses a significant threat to coral reefs. It has been assumed that mesophotic coral ecosystems may serve as refugia from ocean warming. This study examined the acclimation capacity and thermal tolerance of Porites cylindrica and Turbinaria reniformis transplanted to mesophotic depths for 12 months.

MCE-acclimated fragments showed higher thermal thresholds and survival rates, delayed bleaching and less decline in photosynthetic efficiency compared with shallow fragments. Both species maintained high thermal tolerance despite prolonged exposure to cooler mesophotic temperatures.

2024

Susceptibility of Acropora tenuis to consecutive thermal stress

Hazraty-Kari S*, Tavakoli-Kolour P, Nakamura T, Morita M* (2024). Coral Reefs, 43, 4.

Research highlight

Repeated bleaching-level heat stress progressively reduced coral survival, photosynthetic efficiency and algal density. The results demonstrate that consecutive thermal stress can increase coral susceptibility rather than produce increasing resilience.

Variability in thermal stress thresholds of corals across depths

Tavakoli-Kolour P, Sinniger F, Morita M, Nakamura T, Harii S* (2023). Frontiers in Marine Science, 10, 1210662.

Adaptations by the coral Acropora tenuis confer resilience to future thermal stress

Hazraty-Kari S*, Morita M, Tavakoli-Kolour P, Kitanobo S, Nakamura T, Morita M* (2022). Communications Biology, 5, 1–10.

Research highlight

Heat-stressed adult corals produced more numerous but smaller eggs, while their larvae and recruits showed greater survival under elevated temperature. The study provides evidence that parental thermal stress can influence offspring thermal performance, potentially contributing to coral resilience.

2023

Reactions of juvenile coral to three years of consecutive thermal stress

Hazraty-Kari S*, Morita M, Tavakoli-Kolour P, Nakamura T, Harii S* (2023). Science of the Total Environment, 866, 161227.

Research highlight

Repeated annual heat stress progressively reduced juvenile survival and physiological performance. After three consecutive years of stress, survival declined substantially, demonstrating the vulnerability of young corals to repeated heatwaves.

Earlier Research — Persian Gulf

2014

Mass coral bleaching in the northern Persian Gulf, 2012

Kavousi J*, Tavakoli-Kolour P, Mohammadizadeh M, Bahrami A, Barkhordari (2014). Scientia Marina, 78(3).

Research highlight

An exceptionally severe bleaching event affected approximately 84% of surveyed coral colonies. The study estimated a northern Persian Gulf bleaching threshold of approximately 33.5–34 °C and showed that turbid waters did not necessarily protect corals from severe thermal stress.

2015

Outbreak of growth anomalies in coral communities of Qeshm Island, Persian Gulf

Tavakoli-Kolour P*, Kavousi J, Rezai H (2015). International Aquatic Research, 7, 151–156.

Research highlight

The study documented an outbreak of coral growth anomalies in Qeshm Island and identified a strong relationship between colony size and the occurrence of growth anomalies, providing an early baseline for understanding coral disease patterns in the Persian Gulf.

International Scientific Assessment & Policy

2026

Tropical and Subtropical Coral Reefs

Sweet MJ, Beger M, Benzoni F, Camp E, del Campo J, Dobson KL, Galvis N, Ghodrati Shojaei M, Goby G, Harding S, Huang D, Ivanenko V, Richards Z, Rinkevich B, Schläppy ML, Storlazzi CD, Tavakoli-Kolour P, et al. (2026). World Ocean Assessment III, United Nations, New York, USA.

Key points
  • Global coral reef declines continue as temperatures, coastal development, pollution, sedimentation, disease and resource extraction increase.
  • Heatwaves, disease outbreaks and tropical storms are increasing in frequency and severity, reducing recovery time between disturbances.
  • Coral reefs provide major ecological, economic, coastal protection and livelihood benefits.
  • Conservation and restoration require long-term, evidence-based approaches that consider socioeconomic realities and scalability.
  • Global reductions in greenhouse-gas emissions remain essential for maintaining coral reef ecosystems and their functions.
  • The reefs of today will not necessarily be the reefs of tomorrow; conservation goals therefore need to account for shifting ecological baselines.
